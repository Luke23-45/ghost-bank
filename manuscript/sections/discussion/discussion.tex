\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results identify which choices matter within the fixed-total replay
protocol, rather than establishing a universal ranking of class-incremental
methods.  Three observations are especially consequential: the evaluation
readout has the largest measured effect, KD primarily affects retention, and
the total exemplar budget has a larger effect than the tested retrieval budget.

\subsection{What Drives Performance}
\label{subsec:what-drives-performance}

The largest ablation effect arises from replacing nearest-mean exemplar (NME)
evaluation with head-logit evaluation.  This change reduces final average
accuracy by $10.38$ pp and increases forgetting by $32.50$ pp
(Table~\ref{tab:t2-component-ablations}).  The result is consequential because
both variants use the same training procedure and differ only at prediction
time.  In this protocol, the classifier head is therefore not an adequate
substitute for the selected-exemplar prototypes used by NME.  This conclusion
is limited to the trained representation, cosine-margin head, and class order
studied here; it does not imply that head-logit evaluation is unsuitable in all
class-incremental systems.

KD has a different role.  Removing it changes average accuracy by $-0.21$ pp
but raises forgetting by $10.93$ pp.  Thus, under the reported objective, KD
is chiefly a stability mechanism: it preserves prior-task performance without
materially changing the final mean accuracy.  Herding also matters after the
budget and replay procedure are held fixed.  Replacing herding with random
selection reduces average accuracy by $4.83$ pp and increases forgetting by
$4.50$ pp.  By contrast, changing the training head from cosine-margin to
linear has a smaller effect under NME evaluation ($-1.42$ pp accuracy and
$+0.83$ pp forgetting).  Within this experiment, matching the readout to the
exemplar representation and selecting representative exemplars are more
important than this particular head choice.

\subsection{Resource Allocation and Design Implications}
\label{subsec:resource-allocation}

The resource sweep indicates that storage, rather than the number of replay
items drawn per update, is the primary constrained resource over the tested
range.  Reducing the memory from 2,000 to 500 exemplars loses $8.17$ pp in
accuracy and adds $5.77$ pp of forgetting.  Doubling memory from 2,000 to
4,000 improves both metrics, whereas doubling retrieval from 64 to 128 does
not improve either mean metric (Table~\ref{tab:t3-resource-sensitivity}).
These observations do not show that retrieval is irrelevant; they show that,
for this backbone and these budgets, increasing retrieval beyond 64 is not the
first allocation to prioritize.

A practical configuration for the measured setting is consequently: reserve a
fixed total memory budget large enough to retain examples from all observed
classes, rebuild the selected set with herding, train with KD, and evaluate with
NME.  If additional resources are available within the examined range, expanding
memory is better supported by the measurements than increasing the retrieval
budget.  The recommendation is conditional on the stated protocol and should
be re-evaluated when the data stream, architecture, or storage constraint
changes.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

The empirical scope is deliberately narrow.  All experiments use CIFAR-100,
one 10-task class partition induced by split seed 13, and a ResNet-18 backbone.
The three seeds quantify training variation but do not measure sensitivity to
alternative class orders, datasets, architectures, or task granularities.
The paired tests for component ablations are based on three matched seeds and
should be interpreted as supporting evidence, not as high-power estimates of
small effects.

The memory sweep covers 500, 2,000, and 4,000 exemplars.  Although the
increment from 2,000 to 4,000 is smaller than the loss from 2,000 to 500, these
three points do not establish a saturation threshold or a scaling law.  Finally,
the baseline comparison is designed to isolate the replay, selection, and
readout choices in this study; it is not an exhaustive comparison against the
broader class-incremental-learning literature.  Extending the study across
class orders and standard benchmarks, while retaining matched component
ablations, is necessary before making broader method-level claims.
