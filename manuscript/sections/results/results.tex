\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All results use the class-incremental protocol in Section~\ref{sec:method} and
are reported as mean $\pm$ population standard deviation over three seeds.
Table~\ref{tab:t1-master-results} compares methods at the common budget of
2,000 exemplars and 64 replay samples per update.  Tables~\ref{tab:t2-component-ablations}
and~\ref{tab:t3-resource-sensitivity} report matched per-seed deltas for the
ablations and resource sweeps, respectively.

\subsection{Main Comparison}
\label{subsec:main-comparison}

At the common budget, uniform herding obtains $44.99\pm1.00\%$ final average
accuracy and $13.85\pm0.44\%$ forgetting.  Its mean accuracy is 2.63 percentage
points (pp) above iCaRL and 16.39 pp above the static bank; its forgetting is
5.61 pp and 42.01 pp lower, respectively.  The static bank has high accuracy on
the final task ($75.2\pm3.7\%$) but substantially lower accuracy on the earlier
tasks, which is consistent with its high aggregate forgetting.  Figure~\ref{fig:per-task-accuracy}
shows the final per-task accuracies for the baselines and component variants.

\subsection{Component Attribution}
\label{subsec:component-attribution}

The ablations separate the roles of distillation, readout, classifier head, and
exemplar selection (Table~\ref{tab:t2-component-ablations}).  Removing KD changes
mean accuracy by only $-0.21$ pp (not significant in the recorded paired test),
but increases forgetting by $10.93$ pp (significant).  In this configuration,
KD therefore affects retention more strongly than final average accuracy.

Changing the evaluation rule from nearest-mean exemplars to head logits causes
the largest observed degradation: $-10.38$ pp in average accuracy and $+32.50$
pp in forgetting, both significant.  Replacing herding with random selection
reduces average accuracy by $4.83$ pp and increases forgetting by $4.50$ pp,
also with significant paired effects.  Replacing the cosine-margin head with a
linear head gives a smaller $-1.42$ pp accuracy change (marginal) and a
$+0.83$ pp forgetting change (not significant).  These comparisons isolate
effects relative to the locked reference; they do not establish equivalence
between components outside this protocol.

\subsection{Resource Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:resource-sensitivity}

Memory budget has the larger effect in the tested range.  Reducing memory from
2,000 to 500 exemplars lowers average accuracy by $8.17$ pp and raises
forgetting by $5.77$ pp.  Increasing memory to 4,000 raises accuracy by $2.66$
pp and lowers forgetting by $1.18$ pp.  The gain from 2,000 to 4,000 exemplars
is smaller than the loss from 2,000 to 500, indicating diminishing changes over
these three tested budgets rather than a saturation result.

Changing the retrieval budget while keeping 2,000 exemplars has a smaller mean
effect.  Reducing retrieval from 64 to 32 samples changes accuracy by $-1.71$
pp and forgetting by $+1.13$ pp.  Increasing retrieval to 128 does not improve
the mean metrics relative to 64 samples ($-0.18$ pp accuracy and $+0.81$ pp
forgetting).  Figure~\ref{fig:resource-sensitivity} shows these trends and their
seed variation.

\subsection{Accuracy--Forgetting Trade-off}
\label{subsec:accuracy-forgetting-tradeoff}

Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy-forgetting} places all reported configurations in the
accuracy--forgetting plane.  At the shared 2,000-exemplar budget, the reference
has both the highest mean accuracy and the lowest mean forgetting among the
three main methods.  The 4,000-exemplar configuration moves further toward the
upper-left corner, whereas head-logit evaluation and the static bank occupy the
lower-right region.  This plot is descriptive: configurations differ in either
method component or resource budget, so it does not identify a single causal
trade-off curve.

\subsection{Task-Age Failure Modes}
\label{subsec:task-age-failure-modes}

Figure~\ref{fig:forgetting-by-age} exposes two contrasting failure modes.  The
static bank incurs approximately $51$--$59$ pp forgetting for tasks 0--8, while
head-logit evaluation incurs approximately $38$--$55$ pp over the same tasks.
The reference ranges from $7.6$ to $26.8$ pp for these tasks.  In both adverse
configurations, the most recently introduced task has much higher final
accuracy than older tasks (Figure~\ref{fig:per-task-accuracy}), so their final
average accuracy masks a strong recency bias.

The zero forgetting value for task 9 is structural: no later training task can
reduce its accuracy under the final-task definition of forgetting.  It should
therefore not be interpreted as evidence that the last task is retained better
than the preceding tasks.
